NutriMama AI Educational Reference — Calcium & Vitamin D in Pregnancy
(Original educational summary prepared for NutriMama AI, reflecting broadly
published public health consensus. Not a verbatim reproduction of any single
official guideline. Educational use only.)

Why These Nutrients Matter Together
Calcium and vitamin D are closely linked: vitamin D helps the body absorb calcium
from food and supplements. Together they support fetal skeletal development,
maternal bone health, and normal muscle and nerve function. If dietary calcium
intake is insufficient, the body can draw calcium from maternal bone stores to
meet fetal needs, which is one reason adequate intake during pregnancy matters
for long-term maternal bone health as well.

Calcium Targets
A commonly cited target is 1000 mg/day for most pregnant adults, and 1300 mg/day
for pregnant teenagers (under 19), who are often still building peak bone mass
themselves. Good dietary sources include dairy products (milk, yogurt, paneer,
cheese), fortified plant milks, calcium-set tofu, sesame seeds (til), and leafy
greens like collard greens or bok choy (spinach also contains calcium but its
oxalate content reduces how much is actually absorbed).

Vitamin D Targets
A commonly cited target is around 600 IU (15 mcg)/day, though needs can be
individual — factors like limited sun exposure, darker skin tone (which produces
less vitamin D from a given amount of sun exposure), living at higher latitudes,
or consistent use of sunscreen can all affect how much vitamin D the body
synthesizes on its own, and some providers may recommend testing blood levels and
adjusting supplementation accordingly.

Dietary Sources of Vitamin D
Few foods naturally contain much vitamin D. Fatty fish (salmon, mackerel,
sardines), egg yolks, and fortified foods (some milk, plant milks, and cereals)
are among the better dietary sources, but many people — pregnant or not — rely
partly on sunlight exposure and/or supplementation to meet their needs.

Absorption Considerations
- Splitting large calcium doses across the day (rather than one large dose) can
  improve absorption efficiency for some people.
- Calcium can compete with iron for absorption, which is one reason some
  providers suggest taking iron and calcium supplements at different times of day.
- Vitamin D is fat-soluble, so it's typically absorbed better when taken with a
  meal containing some fat.

Signs Worth Discussing With a Provider
Muscle cramps (especially leg cramps, which are common in pregnancy and
sometimes — though not always — related to mineral levels) or any concerns about
bone health, particularly for those with limited dairy intake, limited sun
exposure, or other risk factors, are reasonable topics to raise at a prenatal
visit. This reference is educational and does not replace bloodwork or a
provider's individualized recommendation.

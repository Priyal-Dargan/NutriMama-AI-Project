NutriMama AI Educational Reference — General Pregnancy Nutrition Guidance
(Original educational summary prepared for NutriMama AI. Reflects broadly published
public health consensus on pregnancy nutrition. This is not a verbatim reproduction
of any single official guideline document and is for educational purposes only —
always confirm specifics with your own healthcare provider.)

Energy Needs During Pregnancy
Calorie needs generally do not increase in the first trimester. In the second
trimester, an additional 300-350 kcal per day above a person's pre-pregnancy
baseline is commonly recommended. In the third trimester, this rises to roughly
450-500 kcal per day above baseline. These are averages — actual needs vary with
starting weight, activity level, and whether a pregnancy involves multiples.

Protein
Protein needs increase meaningfully during pregnancy, commonly cited around
71 grams per day, compared to roughly 46 grams for a non-pregnant adult. Protein
supports the growth of fetal tissue, placental development, and increased maternal
blood volume. Good sources include legumes, dairy, eggs, fish (low-mercury
varieties), poultry, and lean meat, along with plant sources like tofu, lentils,
and quinoa for those following a vegetarian or vegan diet.

Folate and Folic Acid
Folate (or its synthetic form, folic acid) is one of the most consistently
emphasized nutrients in pregnancy nutrition guidance, due to its role in preventing
neural tube defects. A commonly cited target is 600 micrograms of dietary folate
equivalents per day during pregnancy, ideally started before conception where
possible. Leafy greens, legumes, citrus fruits, and fortified grains are good
sources, though many providers also recommend a prenatal supplement to reliably
meet this target.

Iron
Iron requirements roughly double during pregnancy to support increased maternal
blood volume and fetal development, with 27 mg/day commonly cited as a target.
Iron-deficiency anemia is common in pregnancy and is usually screened for at
routine prenatal visits. (See the dedicated Iron & Anemia reference document for
more detail.)

Calcium and Vitamin D
Calcium supports fetal skeletal development; a commonly cited target is 1000 mg/day
for most pregnant adults (1300 mg/day for pregnant teenagers). Vitamin D works
alongside calcium for bone health and immune function, with roughly 600 IU
(15 mcg)/day commonly cited, though individual needs can vary, especially with
limited sun exposure.

Omega-3 Fatty Acids (DHA)
DHA, an omega-3 fatty acid, supports fetal brain and eye development, particularly
in the third trimester. Low-mercury fatty fish (like salmon or sardines) 2-3 times
per week is a commonly cited way to meet this need; algae-based DHA supplements
are an alternative for those who don't eat fish.

Hydration
Fluid needs increase during pregnancy to support increased blood volume and
amniotic fluid. A commonly cited general target is around 2.3-3 liters (roughly
10-12 cups) of total fluids per day from all sources, adjusted for climate,
activity, and individual factors.

General Weight Gain Guidance
Recommended total weight gain during pregnancy is often expressed as a range based
on pre-pregnancy BMI:
- Underweight (BMI under 18.5): approximately 12.5-18 kg
- Normal weight (BMI 18.5-24.9): approximately 11.5-16 kg
- Overweight (BMI 25-29.9): approximately 7-11.5 kg
- Obese (BMI 30+): approximately 5-9 kg
These are general population ranges, not individualized medical targets — a
healthcare provider should be consulted for personalized guidance, especially
where multiples, pre-existing conditions, or unusual weight trends are involved.

When to Seek Immediate Medical Attention
Nutrition guidance is never a substitute for urgent medical care. Symptoms such as
heavy vaginal bleeding, severe abdominal pain, persistent severe headache with
vision changes, reduced fetal movement, chest pain, or difficulty breathing warrant
immediate contact with a healthcare provider or emergency services rather than
dietary self-management.

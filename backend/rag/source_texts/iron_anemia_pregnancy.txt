NutriMama AI Educational Reference — Iron & Anemia in Pregnancy
(Original educational summary prepared for NutriMama AI, reflecting broadly
published public health consensus. Not a verbatim reproduction of any single
official guideline. Educational use only — consult your healthcare provider for
personal medical guidance, especially if you have been diagnosed with anemia.)

Why Iron Matters More in Pregnancy
Blood volume increases by roughly 40-50% during pregnancy to support the growing
fetus and placenta. This extra blood requires more iron to produce hemoglobin,
the protein that carries oxygen. Without enough iron, a pregnant person can
develop iron-deficiency anemia, which has been associated with fatigue, reduced
work capacity, and — in more severe or prolonged cases — increased risk of preterm
delivery and low birth weight.

Common Target
A commonly cited target during pregnancy is 27 mg of iron per day, roughly double
the non-pregnant adult target. Many prenatal vitamins are formulated with this in
mind, though individual needs vary and should be confirmed with a provider,
especially for those already diagnosed with anemia, who may be advised a higher
therapeutic dose under medical supervision.

Dietary Sources
Iron comes in two forms:
- Heme iron (from animal sources: red meat, poultry, fish) is generally more
  easily absorbed by the body.
- Non-heme iron (from plant sources: lentils, chickpeas, tofu, spinach, fortified
  cereals, pumpkin seeds) is somewhat less efficiently absorbed on its own, but
  absorption improves significantly when paired with vitamin C-rich foods (citrus,
  tomatoes, bell peppers) in the same meal.

Absorption Tips
- Pairing iron-rich foods with vitamin C sources can meaningfully improve
  non-heme iron absorption.
- Tea and coffee contain tannins that can inhibit iron absorption; some guidance
  suggests spacing these apart from iron-rich meals or iron supplements by roughly
  an hour.
- Calcium can also compete with iron for absorption, which is one reason some
  providers advise taking iron and calcium supplements at different times of day
  rather than together.

Common Symptoms of Iron-Deficiency Anemia
Fatigue, pale skin, shortness of breath, dizziness, and unusual cravings for
non-food items (a condition called pica) are commonly cited symptoms. Because mild
anemia can be asymptomatic, routine blood testing (checking hemoglobin and often
ferritin) during prenatal visits is the standard way it's identified, rather than
relying on symptoms alone.

Supplementation
For those diagnosed with anemia, providers often recommend an iron supplement
dose higher than what's in a standard prenatal vitamin, sometimes alongside
dietary changes. Supplement type, dose, and timing should be determined by a
healthcare provider based on bloodwork — self-prescribing high-dose iron is not
advised, since excess iron can cause gastrointestinal side effects and, in rare
cases, more serious complications.
